# BUS210 Teaching and Course Profile

## Course identity

BUS210 Finance introduces students to the financial reasoning that supports sound business decisions. The course is built around a simple but demanding idea: finance is disciplined decision-making under uncertainty. Students learn to identify relevant cash flows, evaluate their timing and risk, determine value, and use that analysis to make and explain a decision.

The course connects financial theory to business practice through Excel modeling, current-market context, worked examples, applied scenarios, discussion, and independent assessment. It is not simply a course in formulas. It is a course in how to think clearly about money, risk, value, and choice.

## Key attributes of Professor Evitts's teaching style

### Practical and decision-centered

Concepts are taught through the decisions they help students make. Present value is connected to comparing alternatives today; annuities to loans, retirement, and recurring payments; bond and stock valuation to market prices; and WACC, NPV, and IRR to the allocation of business capital. Students are expected to move beyond calculating an answer and explain what that answer means.

### Structured around preparation, practice, and proof

The recurring learning cycle is:

1. **Prepare:** Read or watch the assigned material and arrive with the correct workbook and tools.
2. **Practice:** Build the financial model, work through examples, discuss the reasoning, and interpret the result.
3. **Prove:** Demonstrate independent understanding through a quiz, case, checkpoint, or exam.

This rhythm makes expectations visible while steadily moving students from supported practice to independent performance.

### Active and highly applied

Class time combines concise instruction with worked examples, retrieval practice, discussion, Excel-based exercises, and business scenarios. Students are asked to predict, calculate, compare, check, interpret, and decide. Participation means doing the intellectual work of finance, not merely listening to a lecture.

### Excel-integrated and audit-minded

Excel is treated as part of the language of finance rather than an optional technical add-on. Students build flexible models, use appropriate financial functions, preserve visible formulas, apply correct cash-flow sign conventions, and check whether results are reasonable. Requiring students to show their work reinforces transparency, model discipline, error detection, and professional accountability.

### Connected to markets and professional practice

The course regularly connects classroom concepts to current financial news, interest rates, Federal Reserve policy, capital markets, geopolitical risk, and business events. Professor Evitts's CFA background and extensive investment-management experience give these connections an applied professional perspective. Students learn to separate relevant evidence from market noise and to distinguish disciplined investing from unsupported speculation.

### Rigorous, transparent, and supportive

Expectations for preparation, attendance, deadlines, academic integrity, and independent work are explicit. At the same time, students are encouraged to communicate early when they are struggling, use available support, practice consistently, and be patient with themselves while learning difficult material. The combination is high structure with meaningful support—not reduced standards.

### Designed for student clarity

The course materials emphasize visible learning paths, clear next actions, accessible organization, and consistent navigation. Students can see how individual lessons fit within the semester and what later topics are building toward, even when those lessons are not yet open. This reduces avoidable confusion and helps students take greater responsibility for their progress.

## BUS210 content

### 1. Value money across time

Students begin with the time value of money, the foundation for nearly every later topic in finance. They learn to use cash-flow timelines, financial mathematics, and Excel to analyze:

- present and future value;
- compounding and discounting;
- rates and time periods;
- annuities and perpetuities;
- loans, credit cards, and mortgages;
- retirement and savings decisions; and
- payment, rate, and term comparisons.

### 2. Understand financial markets and institutions

Students examine how money and capital move among savers, borrowers, financial institutions, markets, and businesses. They consider the role of commercial banks, central banks, interest rates, monetary policy, and market information in shaping financing conditions and business decisions.

### 3. Value debt

The debt unit develops students' ability to analyze bonds, yields, interest-rate sensitivity, mortgage cash flows, borrowing costs, and refinancing choices. Students learn why bond prices and market rates move in opposite directions and how required returns translate future contractual cash flows into value today.

### 4. Evaluate equity, risk, and return

Students study common-stock valuation and the relationship between expected return and risk. Major concepts include:

- equity pricing and dividend-based valuation;
- market multiples such as price-to-earnings ratios;
- expected and realized return;
- standard deviation and volatility;
- diversification;
- systematic and unsystematic risk; and
- the Capital Asset Pricing Model and required return.

### 5. Determine the cost of capital and choose investments

The course culminates in the financial logic businesses use to decide which long-term investments create value. Students estimate the weighted average cost of capital and evaluate projects using net present value, internal rate of return, and financial judgment. This unit brings together cash flows, timing, risk, required return, valuation, and strategic choice.

### Skills developed across the course

Throughout BUS210, students practice how to:

- translate a business situation into relevant financial inputs;
- organize cash flows on a consistent timeline;
- select and build an appropriate model;
- calculate accurately by hand and in Excel;
- test assumptions and identify model errors;
- compare alternatives on an economically meaningful basis;
- interpret results in plain business language; and
- defend a recommendation with evidence.

## Why finance is essential for business students

Finance matters because every business function eventually encounters questions about resources, timing, risk, and value. Marketing initiatives require budgets and return expectations. Operations decisions involve assets, capacity, and working capital. Entrepreneurs must understand funding, ownership, cash runway, and growth tradeoffs. Managers must compare projects, negotiate financing, and recognize when reported performance does not equal value creation.

For business students, finance provides several essential capabilities:

### A common language for business decisions

Finance gives students a consistent way to compare choices that occur at different times, carry different risks, or require different levels of investment. Present value, required return, and opportunity cost make unlike alternatives economically comparable.

### Better capital allocation

Businesses have limited money, time, and managerial attention. Students need to understand how firms decide which assets, projects, products, and strategies deserve those scarce resources. Capital budgeting turns strategy into a testable value proposition.

### Stronger risk judgment

Business decisions are made before outcomes are known. Finance helps students identify uncertainty, distinguish diversifiable from market-wide risk, connect greater risk to required return, and avoid treating confidence or attention as evidence.

### Market and economic awareness

Interest rates, inflation, Federal Reserve policy, credit conditions, and investor expectations affect borrowing costs, customer behavior, asset values, and business strategy. Understanding these forces helps future managers interpret the environment in which their organizations operate.

### Quantitative and technological fluency

Excel-based financial modeling develops transferable skills in structuring problems, working with data, documenting assumptions, checking calculations, and communicating outputs. These are valuable well beyond a traditional finance role.

### Personal as well as professional value

Time value of money, loans, mortgages, credit cards, saving, investing, and retirement are also personal decisions. The course equips students to assess financial claims and make more informed choices throughout their lives.

### Responsible leadership

Good financial analysis makes assumptions and tradeoffs visible. It helps leaders explain why a decision creates value, who bears the risk, what could go wrong, and which evidence would change the recommendation. That combination of analysis, transparency, and judgment is central to responsible business leadership.

## The central message of BUS210

BUS210 teaches students to ask three questions whenever they evaluate a financial asset, business project, or strategic choice:

1. What cash flows will this decision generate?
2. What risks are associated with those cash flows?
3. What are those cash flows worth today?

Students who can answer those questions carefully—and support their answers with a transparent model—have learned more than a collection of finance formulas. They have developed a durable framework for making better business decisions.
